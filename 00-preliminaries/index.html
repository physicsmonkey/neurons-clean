Preliminaries: the objects the essay talks about

Neurons, features, the residual stream, and the tools used to look at them

The essay assumes its reader already knows what a neuron is, what a feature is, why anyone would train a sparse autoencoder, and what an attribution graph shows. This page fills that in for someone with a physics background and no interpretability background. It is deliberately concrete: every object is defined by an equation or a procedure, and the vocabulary the essay uses is collected in a glossary at the end.

If you already work in interpretability, skip to the glossary and the map for physicists.

1. Neurons

A trained network is a fixed function f_\theta from inputs to outputs. “Interpretability” in the sense of this essay means explaining how it computes that function in terms of its internal parts, the way a biologist explains an organism in terms of cells and organs rather than in terms of its genome.

The basic internal part is the neuron. In a fully-connected (MLP) layer with input vector x \in \mathbb{R}^d, neuron i has an input weight vector w_i \in \mathbb{R}^d, a bias b_i, and a scalar nonlinearity \phi:

a_i(x) = \phi\big(w_i \cdot x + b_i\big).

The quantity w_i\cdot x + b_i is the preactivation; a_i is the activation. The layer’s output is then a linear map of the activation vector, y = \sum_i a_i\, v_i, where v_i is neuron i’s output weight vector. So each neuron reads one direction (w_i) and writes one direction (v_i), with a scalar nonlinearity in between. The essay’s language of “readers” and “what a neuron lets through” is literal: downstream neurons whose w_j has a large component along v_i are the readers of neuron i.

Three nonlinearities matter here:

  • ReLU, \phi(z) = \max(z, 0). Used in InceptionV1 and most pre-2020 vision models. The negative side of the preactivation is thrown away.
  • GELU, a smooth version of ReLU, \phi(z) = z\,\Phi(z) with \Phi the Gaussian CDF. Used in GPT-2 and ConvNeXt. Slightly negative for small negative z, which is why the essay can talk about a neuron’s negative side even in GELU networks.
  • SwiGLU (gated), used in Llama, Qwen, and most modern language models. The neuron has two input weight vectors, a gate g_i and a value u_i, and a_i(x) = \mathrm{SiLU}(g_i\cdot x)\,(u_i\cdot x), \qquad \mathrm{SiLU}(z) = \frac{z}{1+e^{-z}}. Since \mathrm{SiLU}(z) is slightly negative for z<0, the product is positive when both the gate and the value are negative. The essay calls this the “double-negative regime” and argues it is an architectural artefact, so keep it in mind.

The negative pole. Even with ReLU, the preactivation w_i\cdot x + b_i can be driven strongly negative by some inputs. The essay insists that this negative side is meaningful: the weights w_i are learned as a sum of “votes”, and whatever pushes the preactivation down is, by construction, the thing the neuron is built to not respond to. The essay’s canonical example is a neuron whose positive pole is “V-shaped” and whose negative pole is “round”. It uses the word pole for each side.

Neurons in convolutional networks. In a conv net the “neuron” is a channel: a filter w_i that is applied at every spatial position, giving an activation map a_i(x, \text{pos}) rather than a single number. InceptionV1 neuron “4e:55” means channel 55 of the layer named mixed4e. When the essay shows an image that “activates” a neuron it usually means the maximum, or sum, of the map over positions.

Neurons in transformers. A transformer block has an attention sublayer and an MLP sublayer. The “neurons” are the hidden units of the MLP: for a model with residual width d_{\text{model}} the MLP expands to d_{\text{mlp}} \approx 4\, d_{\text{model}} (or \approx \tfrac{8}{3} d_{\text{model}} for SwiGLU) hidden units, each of which is a neuron in exactly the sense above. There is one activation per neuron per token. Qwen3-32B has 64 layers with 25,600 neurons each, so about 1.6 million neurons, which is the “more than a million neurons” the essay mentions. Attention heads are not neurons and the essay treats them separately.

2. The residual stream

Modern networks (ResNets, ConvNeXt, every transformer) are residual: each layer adds its output to a running vector rather than replacing it,

x_{\ell+1} = x_\ell + f_\ell(x_\ell).

The running vector x_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}} is the residual stream. In a transformer there is one such vector per token position. Every neuron in every layer reads from the stream by taking an inner product with its w_i and writes back to it by adding a multiple of its v_i. The stream is therefore a shared, high-dimensional bus; the only nonlinear operations are the elementwise \phi inside the MLPs, the softmax inside attention, and the normalisation (LayerNorm: subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation across the d_{\text{model}} components, then rescale) that precedes each sublayer.

Two facts about the stream drive much of the essay:

  • It has no privileged basis. Rotating the stream by an orthogonal matrix R, and correspondingly rotating every w_i \to R w_i and v_i \to R v_i, gives an identical network. Nothing about a single coordinate of the stream has any meaning. Neuron activations, by contrast, do have a privileged basis, because \phi acts elementwise: rotating the neurons changes the function. This is why “look at the neurons” is a well-posed instruction and “look at the stream’s coordinates” is not.
  • InceptionV1 has no residual stream (it predates ResNets). Each layer must carry everything the next layer needs. The essay argues that this, rather than any inherent property of learning, is why InceptionV1’s late layers looked like a mess, and it uses the absence of a residual stream to design a squeeze experiment later on.

3. Attention, briefly

An attention head at layer \ell moves information between token positions. For each pair of positions (s, t) it computes a score from the query at t and the key at s, softmaxes the scores over s to get weights A_{ts}, and then adds to the stream at t the weighted sum \sum_s A_{ts}\, W_O W_V\, x_s. The essay’s four questions about a head are: where it looks (A_{ts}, cheap to read off), what it looks for (the query-key circuit), what it does when it finds it (the output-value circuit, which can be read in terms of which neurons’ v_i it picks up and which neurons’ w_j it lands on), and why. The essay only claims progress on the first three and says little about the fourth.

4. Features and the two hypotheses

A feature is, informally, a property of the input that the network represents. The word is notoriously hard to make precise and the essay’s appendix has a note on exactly this problem. The working definition in most of the field is the linear representation hypothesis: a feature is a direction d_k in some activation space, and the degree to which the feature is present in input x is the projection of the activation onto d_k.

If the feature directions happened to coincide with neuron directions, every neuron would respond to exactly one human-recognisable thing. Such a neuron is called monosemantic. A neuron that seems to respond to several unrelated things is polysemantic. The famous example, and the essay’s opening target, is InceptionV1 neuron 4e:55, which the 2020 Zoom In paper reported as responding to both cat faces and car fronts.

Two explanations for polysemanticity were on the table before this essay:

  1. Superposition. The network wants to represent more features than it has neurons, so it assigns features to nearly orthogonal directions that are not neuron-aligned. Each neuron then carries small pieces of several unrelated features. The next page reproduces the toy model that made this precise. Under this hypothesis the neurons are the wrong basis, and the right move is to decompose the activations into the underlying features by some optimisation (a sparse autoencoder).
  2. Generalisation / composition. The neuron really does respond to one thing, but that thing is not a noun we have a word for (“V-shape” rather than “cat or car”), or the neuron grew outward from a core onto related territory during training (“recursive generalisation”). Under this hypothesis the neurons are the right basis and the task is to explain them better.

These make opposite predictions about what should be bundled together: superposition bundles things because they are unrelated (so that they rarely co-occur and interfere little), generalisation bundles things because they are related. The essay’s entire argument is that the evidence points to the second.

5. How people look at neurons

The tools below are the “microscope” the essay says was mis-set. Each is simple to state.

Top-activating examples. Run a large dataset through the network, record each neuron’s activation on every input (or every token), and sort. The top few dozen inputs are the cheapest description of a neuron, and automated labelling pipelines mostly look at these. The essay’s “weak activations” section is about everything below the top of this list.

Feature visualisation. Instead of searching a dataset, synthesise an input by gradient ascent on the activation, x^\star = \arg\max_x a_i(x), starting from noise. Done naively this produces adversarial high-frequency noise. The 2017 Feature Visualization paper made it work by parametrising the image in a decorrelated Fourier basis and by transformation robustness: optimise the expected activation under random jitter, scaling and rotation of the image, so the optimiser must find a pattern that excites the neuron the way a real image would. The essay’s point about “reward hacking” is that in late layers the optimiser still finds a route the network rarely uses on real data (it draws dogs when the real circuit carries car information), and that this is a property of the tool, not the neuron.

Ablation. Set a neuron’s activation to zero (or to its dataset mean) on a given input and measure what changes in the output. This is the interventional test behind statements like “remove the eight neurons with a Santa negative side and the Santa probability falls from near-certain to 6 percent”.

Attribution. For a given input, estimate how much neuron i in one layer contributes to neuron j in a later layer. The simplest version is the product of a_i and the gradient \partial a_j / \partial a_i, which is exact when the path between them is linear. In a residual network the path from v_i to w_j through the stream is exactly linear, so the direct effect is just a_i\,(v_i\cdot w_j), and the only approximation is in how the indirect effects through intervening nonlinearities are handled.

Attribution graphs. Take one prompt, compute attributions between all pairs of units across all layers, keep the strong edges, and draw the result as a graph from input tokens to output logits. Anthropic’s 2025 circuit tracing work builds these graphs on transcoder features rather than neurons. A transcoder is a trained replacement for each MLP with a sparse, wide hidden layer, chosen so that the hidden units are more interpretable than the original neurons. The cost is that the replacement model is only approximately the original: the essay quotes Anthropic’s own methods page for the figure that it predicts the same next token only about half the time. The essay’s language-model claim is that the same graphs can be built on the original neurons with no replacement model at all.

Steering. Add a multiple of v_i to the stream during generation and see what the model does. Used in the essay as an informal check (“steering the Colorado neuron makes the model want to go hiking”).

6. Sparse autoencoders (dictionary learning)

If superposition is true, activation vectors x \in \mathbb{R}^d are sparse non-negative combinations of n \gg d feature directions, x \approx \sum_k f_k(x)\, d_k with few f_k \neq 0. A sparse autoencoder (SAE) tries to recover the dictionary \{d_k\} and the coefficients f_k by training

f(x) = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}}\, x + b_{\text{enc}}), \qquad \hat x = W_{\text{dec}}\, f(x) + b_{\text{dec}},

to minimise \|x - \hat x\|^2 + \lambda \|f(x)\|_1 over a large dataset of activations. The \ell_1 penalty enforces sparsity; the columns of W_{\text{dec}} are the learned feature directions. A TopK SAE replaces the penalty by keeping only the k largest preactivations per input, which is what the essay uses (640 features, k=2). “Dictionary learning” is the general name for this family.

Two numbers are always reported. Variance explained says how well \hat x reconstructs x. More honest is to splice the SAE into the network, replacing x by \hat x at that layer, and measure how much the network’s accuracy or loss degrades, because small reconstruction error can still destroy information the network needed. The essay reports both for its squeeze experiment.

The essay is not against SAEs as such. Its claim is that, in the networks it studied, an SAE mostly rediscovers the neurons (88 percent of SAE activity on ConvNeXt’s last layer is a near copy of a single neuron) and the rest is loose regroupings of them, and that on InceptionV1 4e:55 the SAE “fractures” a single V-shape neuron into a car feature and a cat feature, which is further from what the neuron does.

7. The model zoo

The essay moves between several networks. A one-line sketch of each:

Model Year Size Type Why it appears
InceptionV1 (GoogLeNet) 2014 6.6 M params conv net, no residual stream, ReLU The original model organism; layers named mixed3a … mixed5b
ConvNeXt (small / medium / large) 2022 design, trained by the author 92 k / 7.06 M / 28.6 M modern conv net with residuals, LayerNorm, GELU A 300× size sweep at fixed architecture; medium matches InceptionV1’s parameter count
CLIP 2021 ~ 400 M image–text contrastive model Source of the “multimodal neurons” paper
DINOv2 2023 ~ 86 M (ViT-B) self-supervised vision transformer Shows the same pose neuron appears at 10× InceptionV1’s size
GPT-2 XL 2019 1.5 B transformer, GELU Model of the 2023 OpenAI automated-interpretability project
Qwen3-32B 2025 32 B dense transformer, SwiGLU Main language model in the essay
Qwen3.6 (MoE) 2026 — mixture of experts The 10,240-expert survey
Kimi K3 2026 > 1 T mixture of experts “First attribution graph of a multi-trillion parameter model”

Mixture of experts. In an MoE transformer the MLP sublayer is replaced by many independent MLPs (“experts”) and a learned router that sends each token to a few of them. Each expert has its own neurons. The essay argues experts are far more specialised than usually reported and calls the architecture “anti-superposition”, because the router would have to recognise thousands of distinct things for packing to occur.

8. A map for physicists

Some intuitions carry over cleanly; others actively mislead. A short list.

  • Superposition is a compressed-sensing statement. n sparse signals in d < n dimensions can be stored and recovered if their directions are nearly orthogonal (Johnson–Lindenstrauss: one can fit e^{O(\epsilon^2 d)} vectors in \mathbb{R}^d with pairwise overlaps below \epsilon). The toy model on the next page is a learned version of this with ReLU as the decoder. The essay accepts the mathematics and disputes whether real networks are in the regime where the trade-off (capacity vs interference) is the dominant consideration.
  • “Circuit racing” is mode competition. The essay’s account of why networks generalise is that broad features fire on more of the data, receive more gradient, and starve the narrow ones. This is rich-get-richer dynamics in the sense of a linear instability: whichever direction has the largest growth rate dominates. The companion idea in the literature is “gradient starvation” and “coherent gradients”.
  • The privileged basis is a symmetry statement. The residual stream has an O(d) symmetry; the neuron layer breaks it to permutations (and sign flips, in some cases) because \phi is elementwise. Asking which basis is “real” is asking which symmetry the dynamics breaks.
  • Recursive generalisation vs superposition is a locality question. Generalisation is local in some similarity metric on the data; superposition is non-local by design. This is the one empirical dichotomy the essay turns on, and watching a neuron over training checkpoints is the proposed experiment.
  • Beware “feature” as a conserved quantity. The essay’s appendix argues that the toy model prices the cost of sharing a neuron (interference) but not the benefit (reuse by downstream readers), because in the toy there is nothing downstream. In a real network a representation only matters through what reads it. A physicist’s instinct to count degrees of freedom and ask whether n > d is, on the essay’s account, the wrong question.
  • Beware the “microscope” analogy as reassurance. A real microscope’s artefacts are well understood; the essay’s whole historical section is about artefacts in feature visualisation and in the choice of model organism that went unrecognised for years.

Glossary

Terms in the essay’s own sense, in roughly the order they appear.

  • Polysemantic / monosemantic. A neuron that appears to respond to several unrelated things / to one thing. The essay argues the first is largely an observer’s statement.
  • Superposition. Unrelated features stored in overlapping, non-neuron-aligned directions because the network has run out of neurons. The essay distinguishes three senses: (a) more features than dimensions, which a clean network could also satisfy; (b) a learning principle that packs unrelated things into neurons, which is what the essay argues against; (c) the same principle applied to directions in the residual stream.
  • Recursive generalisation. A neuron starts on one core and sprouts related offshoots as training proceeds, “the way a tree branch sprouts sub-branches”. Earlier called “conjoined features” in the multimodal-neurons paper.
  • Pole. The positive or negative side of a neuron’s preactivation. Both are treated as meaningful.
  • Gate. A neuron viewed as control flow: what it lets through and what it keeps out, rather than what noun it names.
  • Circuit. A set of neurons across layers connected by large weights, doing an identifiable sub-computation.
  • Motif. A pattern that recurs across many circuits (e.g. “summary on punctuation”, “say or just think”, pose neurons feeding class detectors).
  • Weak activations. Everything below the top of a neuron’s activation distribution; the essay argues they are mostly generalisation and mostly ablatable.
  • Blind maximum. Test an explanation by having a second agent, who sees only the explanation, write an input intended to maximally activate the neuron. If it beats the dataset maximum the explanation captured the centre of the neuron.
  • Neuron paper. A short written audit of one neuron, produced by an agent; the essay reports statistics over 1,000 of them.
  • Attribution graph. A per-prompt graph of which units drive which, from tokens to logits.
  • Enumerative vs exploratory interpretability. Build a complete list of features to audit, vs start from questions and move through the network as needed. The essay favours the second and says the first was “always a means to an end”.
  • Already clean. The essay’s thesis: the network’s own parts are built to be read by its own downstream parts, and are therefore readable by us without being decomposed first.

## 1. Neurons

A trained network is a fixed function $f_\theta$ from inputs to outputs. "Interpretability" in
the sense of this essay means explaining *how* it computes that function in terms of its
internal parts, the way a biologist explains an organism in terms of cells and organs rather
than in terms of its genome.

The basic internal part is the **neuron**. In a fully-connected (MLP) layer with input vector
$x \in \mathbb{R}^d$, neuron $i$ has an input weight vector $w_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$, a bias
$b_i$, and a scalar nonlinearity $\phi$:

$$
a_i(x) = \phi\big(w_i \cdot x + b_i\big).
$$

The quantity $w_i\cdot x + b_i$ is the **preactivation**; $a_i$ is the **activation**. The
layer's output is then a linear map of the activation vector, $y = \sum_i a_i\, v_i$, where
$v_i$ is neuron $i$'s **output weight** vector. So each neuron *reads* one direction
($w_i$) and *writes* one direction ($v_i$), with a scalar nonlinearity in between. The essay's
language of "readers" and "what a neuron lets through" is literal: downstream neurons whose

- **ReLU**, $\phi(z) = \max(z, 0)$. Used in InceptionV1 and most pre-2020 vision models. The

  Used in GPT-2 and ConvNeXt. Slightly negative for small negative $z$, which is why the essay
  can talk about a neuron's negative side even in GELU networks.
- **SwiGLU** (gated), used in Llama, Qwen, and most modern language models. The neuron has two
  input weight vectors, a *gate* $g_i$ and a *value* $u_i$, and
  $$
  a_i(x) = \mathrm{SiLU}(g_i\cdot x)\,(u_i\cdot x), \qquad \mathrm{SiLU}(z) = \frac{z}{1+e^{-z}}.

  the gate and the value are negative. The essay calls this the "double-negative regime" and
  argues it is an architectural artefact, so keep it in mind.

still finds a route the network rarely uses on real data (it draws dogs when the real
circuit carries car information), and that this is a property of the tool, not the neuron.

**Ablation.** Set a neuron's activation to zero (or to its dataset mean) on a given input
and measure what changes in the output. This is the interventional test behind statements
like "remove the eight neurons with a Santa negative side and the Santa probability falls
from near-certain to 6 percent".

**Attribution.** For a given input, estimate how much neuron $i$ in one layer contributes to
neuron $j$ in a later layer. The simplest version is the product of $a_i$ and the gradient
$\partial a_j / \partial a_i$, which is exact when the path between them is linear. In a
residual network the path from $v_i$ to $w_j$ *through the stream* is exactly linear, so the
direct effect is just $a_i\,(v_i\cdot w_j)$, and the only approximation is in how the
indirect effects through intervening nonlinearities are handled.

**Attribution graphs.** Take one prompt, compute attributions between all pairs of units
across all layers, keep the strong edges, and draw the result as a graph from input tokens
to output logits. Anthropic's 2025 *circuit tracing* work builds these graphs on
**transcoder** features rather than neurons. A transcoder is a trained replacement for each
MLP with a sparse, wide hidden layer, chosen so that the hidden units are more interpretable
than the original neurons. The cost is that the replacement model is only approximately the
original: the essay quotes Anthropic's own methods page for the figure that it predicts the
same next token only about half the time. The essay's language-model claim is that the same
graphs can be built on the original neurons with no replacement model at all.

**Steering.** Add a multiple of $v_i$ to the stream during generation and see what the model
does. Used in the essay as an informal check ("steering the Colorado neuron makes the model
want to go hiking").

## 6. Sparse autoencoders (dictionary learning)

If superposition is true, activation vectors $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are sparse non-negative
combinations of $n \gg d$ feature directions, $x \approx \sum_k f_k(x)\, d_k$ with few
$f_k \neq 0$. A **sparse autoencoder** (SAE) tries to recover the dictionary $\{d_k\}$ and the
coefficients $f_k$ by training

$$
f(x) = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}}\, x + b_{\text{enc}}), \qquad
\hat x = W_{\text{dec}}\, f(x) + b_{\text{dec}},
$$

to minimise $\|x - \hat x\|^2 + \lambda \|f(x)\|_1$ over a large dataset of activations. The
$\ell_1$ penalty enforces sparsity; the columns of $W_{\text{dec}}$ are the learned feature
directions. A **TopK** SAE replaces the penalty by keeping only the $k$ largest
preactivations per input, which is what the essay uses (640 features, $k=2$). "Dictionary
learning" is the general name for this family.

Two numbers are always reported. **Variance explained** says how well $\hat x$ reconstructs
$x$. More honest is to **splice** the SAE into the network, replacing $x$ by $\hat x$ at that
layer, and measure how much the network's accuracy or loss degrades, because small
reconstruction error can still destroy information the network needed. The essay reports
both for its squeeze experiment.

The essay is not against SAEs as such. Its claim is that, in the networks it studied, an SAE
mostly rediscovers the neurons (88 percent of SAE activity on ConvNeXt's last layer is a near
copy of a single neuron) and the rest is loose regroupings of them, and that on InceptionV1
4e:55 the SAE "fractures" a single V-shape neuron into a car feature and a cat feature, which
is further from what the neuron does.

## 7. The model zoo

The essay moves between several networks. A one-line sketch of each:

| Model | Year | Size | Type | Why it appears |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| InceptionV1 (GoogLeNet) | 2014 | 6.6 M params | conv net, no residual stream, ReLU | The original model organism; layers named `mixed3a … mixed5b` |
| ConvNeXt (small / medium / large) | 2022 design, trained by the author | 92 k / 7.06 M / 28.6 M | modern conv net with residuals, LayerNorm, GELU | A 300× size sweep at fixed architecture; medium matches InceptionV1's parameter count |
| CLIP | 2021 | ~ 400 M | image–text contrastive model | Source of the "multimodal neurons" paper |
| DINOv2 | 2023 | ~ 86 M (ViT-B) | self-supervised vision transformer | Shows the same pose neuron appears at 10× InceptionV1's size |
| GPT-2 XL | 2019 | 1.5 B | transformer, GELU | Model of the 2023 OpenAI automated-interpretability project |
| Qwen3-32B | 2025 | 32 B | dense transformer, SwiGLU | Main language model in the essay |
| Qwen3.6 (MoE) | 2026 | — | mixture of experts | The 10,240-expert survey |
| Kimi K3 | 2026 | > 1 T | mixture of experts | "First attribution graph of a multi-trillion parameter model" |

**Mixture of experts.** In an MoE transformer the MLP sublayer is replaced by many
independent MLPs ("experts") and a learned router that sends each token to a few of them.
Each expert has its own neurons. The essay argues experts are far more specialised than
usually reported and calls the architecture "anti-superposition", because the router would
have to recognise thousands of distinct things for packing to occur.

## 8. A map for physicists {#a-map-for-physicists}

Some intuitions carry over cleanly; others actively mislead. A short list.

- **Superposition is a compressed-sensing statement.** $n$ sparse signals in $d < n$
  dimensions can be stored and recovered if their directions are nearly orthogonal
  (Johnson–Lindenstrauss: one can fit $e^{O(\epsilon^2 d)}$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ with
  pairwise overlaps below $\epsilon$). The toy model on the next page is a learned version of
  this with ReLU as the decoder. The essay accepts the mathematics and disputes whether real
  networks are in the regime where the trade-off (capacity vs interference) is the dominant
  consideration.
- **"Circuit racing" is mode competition.** The essay's account of why networks generalise is
  that broad features fire on more of the data, receive more gradient, and starve the narrow
  ones. This is rich-get-richer dynamics in the sense of a linear instability: whichever
  direction has the largest growth rate dominates. The companion idea in the literature is
  "gradient starvation" and "coherent gradients".
- **The privileged basis is a symmetry statement.** The residual stream has an $O(d)$
  symmetry; the neuron layer breaks it to permutations (and sign flips, in some cases)
  because $\phi$ is elementwise. Asking which basis is "real" is asking which symmetry the
  dynamics breaks.
- **Recursive generalisation vs superposition is a locality question.** Generalisation is
  local in some similarity metric on the data; superposition is non-local by design. This is
  the one empirical dichotomy the essay turns on, and watching a neuron over training
  checkpoints is the proposed experiment.
- **Beware "feature" as a conserved quantity.** The essay's appendix argues that the toy
  model prices the *cost* of sharing a neuron (interference) but not the *benefit* (reuse by
  downstream readers), because in the toy there is nothing downstream. In a real network a
  representation only matters through what reads it. A physicist's instinct to count degrees
  of freedom and ask whether $n > d$ is, on the essay's account, the wrong question.
- **Beware the "microscope" analogy as reassurance.** A real microscope's artefacts are well
  understood; the essay's whole historical section is about artefacts in feature
  visualisation and in the choice of model organism that went unrecognised for years.

## Glossary {#glossary}

Terms in the essay's own sense, in roughly the order they appear.

- **Polysemantic / monosemantic.** A neuron that appears to respond to several unrelated
  things / to one thing. The essay argues the first is largely an observer's statement.
- **Superposition.** Unrelated features stored in overlapping, non-neuron-aligned directions
  because the network has run out of neurons. The essay distinguishes three senses: (a)
  more features than dimensions, which a clean network could also satisfy; (b) a learning
  principle that packs unrelated things into neurons, which is what the essay argues
  against; (c) the same principle applied to directions in the residual stream.
- **Recursive generalisation.** A neuron starts on one core and sprouts related offshoots as
  training proceeds, "the way a tree branch sprouts sub-branches". Earlier called "conjoined
  features" in the multimodal-neurons paper.
- **Pole.** The positive or negative side of a neuron's preactivation. Both are treated as
  meaningful.
- **Gate.** A neuron viewed as control flow: what it lets through and what it keeps out,
  rather than what noun it names.
- **Circuit.** A set of neurons across layers connected by large weights, doing an
  identifiable sub-computation.
- **Motif.** A pattern that recurs across many circuits (e.g. "summary on punctuation",
  "say or just think", pose neurons feeding class detectors).
- **Weak activations.** Everything below the top of a neuron's activation distribution; the
  essay argues they are mostly generalisation and mostly ablatable.
- **Blind maximum.** Test an explanation by having a second agent, who sees only the
  explanation, write an input intended to maximally activate the neuron. If it beats the
  dataset maximum the explanation captured the centre of the neuron.
- **Neuron paper.** A short written audit of one neuron, produced by an agent; the essay
  reports statistics over 1,000 of them.
- **Attribution graph.** A per-prompt graph of which units drive which, from tokens to logits.
- **Enumerative vs exploratory interpretability.** Build a complete list of features to
  audit, vs start from questions and move through the network as needed. The essay favours
  the second and says the first was "always a means to an end".
- **Already clean.** The essay's thesis: the network's own parts are built to be read by its
  own downstream parts, and are therefore readable by us without being decomposed first.
